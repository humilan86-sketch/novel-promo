Chapter One: The Attic

For three years I thought I was the luckiest woman in this house.

My husband Daniel was an architect. Good temper, good salary, home before eight every night. His mother lived downstairs and cooked for us every single day. The neighbors said I must have saved a galaxy in a past life.

Then one Tuesday afternoon I came home early to grab a contract, and I heard footsteps above my head.

The attic was locked. Daniel said it was full of old furniture, too dusty to go up. In three years I had never once been up there.

I stood at the bottom of the stairs with the unsigned contract still in my hand. The floorboards creaked above me, the way a chair creaks when someone shifts their weight.

"Daniel?" I called.

The footsteps stopped. The house went so quiet I could hear the refrigerator humming.

I called him. He picked up on the seventh ring.

"Are you home?" His voice was perfectly steady.

"No. I'm at the office." I said. "What time are you back tonight?"

"Around nine. Working late."

I hung up and looked up at the locked attic door.

The key was in the second drawer of his mother's dresser, wrapped in electrical tape. It took me three minutes to find it. The lock looked brand new.

I opened the door, and the smell hit me: perfume and old wood.

There was no old furniture in that attic.

There was a bed, made neatly with a pink duvet. A glass of water on the nightstand, the surface still trembling.

On the dressing table stood a row of skincare products. The newest one was the same bottle the counter girl had told me last month was the last one in the city.

Mine was still in the bathroom, unopened.

I knelt down and found a storage box under the bed. A sticky note was on top, in neat handwriting: clothes, do not touch.

That was Daniel's handwriting.

Inside were dresses, cardigans, pajamas. All one size too small for me.

Underneath everything was a photograph.

Daniel had his arm around a woman, standing in front of my father's grave.

I knew her. Her name was Mira. She had been my father's student. On the day he died five years ago, she knelt in the funeral hall and cried harder than I did. Everyone said it was devotion to a teacher.

On the back of the photograph, someone had written: our third wedding anniversary.

The date was tomorrow.

I sat on the floor and suddenly remembered something my mother-in-law said at our wedding: "From today, this family is finally complete."

I thought she meant us. Now I understood. She wasn't filling in my place. She was filling in someone else's.

I took out my phone and texted Daniel: something came up, I won't be home tonight.

Then I climbed onto that bed with the pink duvet and lay down, staring at the ceiling, waiting to see how they would walk in tomorrow evening.

Chapter Two: The Woman Upstairs

At five past five the next afternoon, I hid in the storage closet under the attic stairs.

Through the gap in the door, I could see the landing perfectly.

Daniel came home early, four hours earlier than usual. He didn't turn on a light. His steps on the stairs were soft, careful, the way you walk when you don't want to wake someone.

At six, the doorbell rang.

A woman's voice floated up the stairwell. "Daniel, I'm here."

It was a soft voice, a little hoarse, and it had the exact cadence of my father's lectures.

My stomach turned over.

When they came upstairs I saw her ankles. There was a scar on her left ankle, in exactly the same place as the scar on mine.

A year ago Daniel took me to a hot spring. When we came back I had a burn on my ankle. He said I had bumped into a heating pipe.

But the scar was shaped like a leaf.

My father used to draw leaves in the margins of his students' papers.

At eleven that night they finally went downstairs. I crawled out, slipped into the attic, and lay down under the bed with my phone recording.

They came up again.

"When are you going to tell her?" the woman asked.

"Not yet," Daniel said, very quietly. "The way she is right now, I'm afraid of what she might do."

"It's been three years. I've waited three years, Daniel. I'm running out of patience."

"I know." He laughed, a laugh I had never heard from him. "Give me one more month. After she signs the equity transfer, we'll go."

"So it's the money."

"It's so I don't regret this for the rest of my life."

I lay under that bed with my nails digging into the floorboards.

Three years of soup. Three years of tenderness. Three years of "I love you" — all of it written into a contract I hadn't signed yet.

Chapter Three: The Letter

I waited until they were asleep, then crawled out.

In my own room I opened the box of my father's things. The lawyer had handed it to me five years ago and I had never dared to open it.

At the bottom was a letter he never sent. It was addressed to me.

"Dear Emma," it began. "If you are reading this, I am gone. The thing I regret most in this life is your mother. But there is something else you should know.

Eight years ago I adopted a girl from the orphanage. She has the same birthday as you. Her name is Mira. I never told you because I was afraid you would feel that part of you had been taken away. She is your sister."

I stared at those last two words. My hands would not stop shaking.

Outside the window, the sky was getting light.

The woman in the attic was my father's other daughter.

And Daniel? Had she chosen him, or had he chosen the money?

I went downstairs, used the little brass key to lock the attic door from the outside, and called my mother-in-law.

"Mom," I said. "I'm making breakfast today."

She was silent for a long moment.

"You know," she said.

"I know."

"That girl..." She paused. "I was the one who let her live up there."

I held the phone and listened to the kettle start to whistle.

I smiled.

"Mom," I said. "Guess where I am right now."

Chapter Four: Breakfast

I stood in the kitchen, frying eggs with my phone propped against the sink, recording everything.

My mother-in-law came down in the robe I had bought her. She saw me and stopped.

"Emma."

"Sit down, Mom." I pushed the plate toward her. "Eat. It's better hot."

She didn't move.

I pulled out a chair and set the photograph on the table.

She glanced at it. Her hand trembled.

"He's not my son," she said suddenly.

I looked up.

"Daniel isn't my biological son." She stared at the photo. "Thirty years ago, in the hospital, they gave me the wrong baby. His real mother was your father's first wife."

My chopsticks slid off the table.

"So Daniel and Mira..."

"There's no blood between them," she said. "Don't think that. But Emma, this family was wrong from the very beginning."

Outside the window, the sky was fully bright.

I finished the fried egg, stood up, and put the equity transfer agreement in front of her.

"Sign it, Mom," I said. "When you do, this house is yours."

She froze.

"I only want one thing."

"Tell me."

I took the brass key out of my pocket and laid it on top of the agreement.

"Those two up there? After last night, I have the recording."

Epilogue

A week later I moved out of that house.

The house, the savings, the shares — I left all of it to my mother-in-law. My lawyer said I was insane.

I said those things were never mine to begin with.

I took exactly one box with me.

Inside were my father's manuscripts, the letter, and a family photo.

In the picture, my father stands in the middle. On his left are my mother and me. On his right is a little girl with braids, smiling politely.

On the back, someone had written: Emma and Mira, eighth birthday.

I put the photo in a frame and set it on the windowsill of my new apartment.

My phone buzzed.

Daniel: Did you report your mother?

Me: No. I just did what my father should have done years ago.

He didn't reply for a long time.

Half an hour later he sent a voice message. I didn't open it. I blocked him.

Rain started falling outside.

I opened my laptop and renamed the recording file: evidence.

Then I stared at the screen and suddenly laughed out loud.

Because I had finally understood something my father had written in that letter.

The last line said: "Emma, I left money that is not in anyone's name. The password is your and Mira's birthday."

I picked up my phone and dialed the one number I had never called.

It rang twice. A soft voice answered.

"Hello?"

I took a deep breath.

"Mira? This is Emma."
